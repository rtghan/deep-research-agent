Deep Research Agent

“Never let a claim outlive the evidence for it.”

A research agent whose claims are not append-only — they are re-attacked, revised, reversed, and retracted as evidence accumulates.

Open: "I'll show you a system, and then I'll show you six experiments — four of which proved me wrong. The second part is the more useful half."
The failure mode I built against

Most research agents are append-only

Round 1 extracts a claim. Rounds 2–N pile more text next to it. Nothing goes back and asks whether claim #4 is still true given what round 3 found.

What the old system did when round 3 contradicted a round-1 claim: a second, peer claim appeared beside it. The contradiction detector noticed the pair at the very end and applied a flat 0.3 confidence penalty to both.

Nobody ever said “claim_3 was wrong; here is the corrected version.”

That is the “comfortable, wrong answer” the brief names. The interesting problem in deep research isn’t retrieval — it’s revision.

Have the real before/after RAG claim pair ready as the concrete anchor.

Agent boundaries: a seam exists where the objective differs

AgentOptimises for
Extractorcoverage
Verifierentailment — does the cited text support this?
Challengerwarrant — is this sound given the whole pool?
Report criticdid we answer the question that was asked?

The verifier/challenger split is the sharp one: a claim can restate one cherry-picked chunk faithfully — support 1.0 — and still be an unsound overgeneralization. support_score cannot express that; reasoning_score is a separate axis.

The anti-case

No debate ensemble. Cost multiplies by N; the extractor→verifier split is already an author–reviewer pattern at the claim level, which is where the errors are. Plain Python, no framework — ~1,500 lines, pipeline.py readable end-to-end. Cost: no durable execution, no resume.

Full design · 3 of 7 · core slide

Claim evolution — the router is arithmetic

balance = (supporting − refuting) / (supporting + refuting), counted by distinct source, not chunk — one paper split into forty chunks must not outvote three papers that disagree.

ConditionOperation
balance > 0.5 · reasoning ≥ 0.6keep
balance > 0.5 · reasoning < 0.6refine
−0.3 ≤ balance ≤ 0.5narrow
balance < −0.3 (and ≥ 2 distinct sources)reverse
balance < −0.3 + verdict unsupportedretract

Why arithmetic: an aggressive critic must not flip a well-supported claim by being loud. The reason a claim reversed is a number in the trace, and evolution’s aggressiveness is a config knob rather than a prompt rewrite.

Asymmetry: the reviser may escalate to retract, but cannot downgrade an assigned reversal into something more convenient.
Budget 3 minutes here alone. This is the contribution.
Full design · 4 of 7

Memory & context

  • State lives in Pydantic, not a context window. Each agent sees only its own inputs; the orchestrator decides what is visible. No agent ever sees full pipeline state. Zero context-overflow errors across every run.
  • Compaction where it matters. The query reformulator never sees the evidence pool (hundreds of chunks, growing every round) — only a digest of prior queries, returned source titles, and the weakest standing claims. A summary of “what we did and what it got us” is cheaper and more actionable than the text it summarises.
  • What agents deliberately do NOT share: sub-questions share nothing. If sq_1 retrieves a paper relevant to sq_3, sq_3 re-retrieves it. Keeps them parallelisable; wasteful.
Flagged before you find it: evidence selection into the challenger prompt is round-robin by source, not semantic. Pairs with the embeddings gap on slide 18.
Full design · 5 of 7

Tools & failure — the real story is boundary bugs

arXiv primary (no key, PDF-first with abstract fallback); web secondary via Tavily → DuckDuckGo → Wikipedia. PDF extraction degrades to '' rather than crashing.

Three real-model failures a mock could never produce

  1. gemma-4-26b returned a bare JSON list where an object was requested → 'list' object has no attribute 'get'
  2. deepseek-chat returned HTTP 200 with choices: None — a genuinely incomplete response body
  3. Rate limits crashed two full runs — no retry/backoff exists

Fixed once at the LLMClient boundary, not in the six call sites: non-dict parses wrap to {"_unparsed": …} so every .get(k, default) degrades to its default.

The meta-finding: a mock can simulate malformed content but not a malformed envelope. This class of bug only appears under real load.
Full design · 6 of 7

Phase 5 — two tiers of error detection

The only stage that asks whether the assembled report answers the question asked. Everything upstream is per-claim.

Tier 1 — mechanical, no LLM

  • retracted claim still asserted
  • sub-question with zero surviving claims
  • thin sub-question (avg conf < 0.45)
  • contradictions found but never discussed

Tier 2 — independent critic

accept · revise_report · needs_more_research

Tier 1 findings are handed to the critic as established facts, so the model spends attention on judgement calls instead of re-deriving what a substring check proved.

The join: a needs_more_research gap is written into retrieval_attempts[-1].gap_noted — which the reformulator already consumes. Critic says what’s missing → reformulator turns it into a different query. Report-level correction and retrieval-level learning are one loop, not two features.

MVP boundary — what I cut, and why that was right

Built and measured

  • Claim evolution + arithmetic routing
  • Quote-grounded refutation
  • Report self-correction (2 tiers)
  • Query reformulation + compaction
  • Live process narration
  • Six ablations, 82 offline checks

Designed, deliberately not built

  • Parallel sub-questions · resume · caching
  • Learned calibration
  • Embedding retrieval / reranking
  • Tool router
  • Prompt-injection defence
  • Multi-modal output

Every cut bought time for measurement instead of surface area. The prioritisation is the thing being graded — and I would make the same call again.

Evaluation · 1 of 6

How do you evaluate with no ground truth?

A layered proxy stack, each layer with a stated failure mode:

  1. Mechanical checks — no LLM; the only layer that cannot be wrong about what it measures
  2. Verifier support_score — but conflates “more accurate” with “harder to fully entail”
  3. Blind, order-randomised pairwise judge — a structurally different question, so it cannot inherit that conflation
  4. Paired ablation + McNemar’s exact test — isolates one variable
Name the circularity before they do: verifier, challenger and judge are all LLMs, and the judge reuses the challenger’s client. No independent ground truth exists anywhere in the stack.
support_lift turned out to measure the wrong thing — caught by triangulating with a second metric. Appendix A4.
Evaluation · 2 of 6

Track A — and the baseline that changes the answer

The original claim: adaptive 98% support @ 31K tokens vs. uniform 99% @ 110K — 3.5× cheaper. That uniform baseline was 4 rounds on everything — the maximum.

My own decision log flagged the missing test: “we did not test a minimum-compute baseline (1 round for all).” So I ran it.

ArmSupportECETokens
uniform — 1 round each100.0%0.2031.85M
threshold (shipped)98.4%0.2303.22M
scheduler (new)98.3%0.2212.58M

The least adaptive arm won on every metric and cost the least.

Both claims are true. Adaptive sits between two baselines: 3.5× cheaper than max-uniform, 1.74× more expensive than min-uniform. Support rate is saturated at 98–100%, so the metric cannot discriminate — and the cheapest arm wins by default.

This is as much a test-design finding as a system finding. The suite was built to stress claim evolution, not compute allocation.
Evaluation · 3 of 6

Track B — I found a bug and mistook it for a result

Three different ECE numbers across the repo looked like an inconsistency. Lining them up looked like a finding: calibration had halved. I wrote that down as “claim evolution improved calibration as a side effect.”

Then I ran the clean ablation — recomputing confidence over the same 582 stored claims, which removes every confound but the formula:

FormulaECEmax conf
F1 — old 2-signal, weights sum to 0.80.3270.80
F2 — same two signals, rescaled to 1.00.1631.00
F3 — full 4-signal (shipped)0.1621.00
F4 — 4-signal minus reasoning0.1531.00

The ceiling accounts for 100% of the gain. F1→F2 = −0.164. F2→F3, the entire contribution of both evolution signals, = −0.0004.

A system right 92–100% of the time was structurally incapable of saying so — weights summing to 0.8. And dropping reasoning_score makes calibration better.

The honest version of this slide: I found a bug, mistook it for a result, then ran the ablation that told me which it was.

Track C — does evolution actually improve claims?

570 revisions across 7 test cases. Blind judge: 425 improved (81.7%), 93 worse (17.9%), 2 same.

The fix that mattered, and why it generalises

Refuting evidence must carry a verbatim quote, mechanically checked as a substring of the cited chunk. 159 of 715 challenges (22.2%) had a refuting citation dropped for failing that check — one in five challenges was refuting on evidence that didn’t say what was claimed.

The prompt already told the model not to treat silence as refutation. It did it anyway.

Counterweight: reverse still makes things worse ~1 time in 5, and refine fired once in 570 revisions under every threshold configuration tested — a branch that cannot fire is a branch that shouldn’t exist.
Evaluation · 5 of 6 · the result I did not want

I tested my core design assumption. It came back false.

The whole reason the challenger runs on a different model: a model critiquing its own claims should go easy on them. Three consecutive evaluations named this the largest unverified claim in the system.

Design the clean test, and reject the easy one. The repo already had an evolution_self ablation — two full pipeline runs. Rejected: different runs retrieve different evidence, confounding challenger identity with different inputs. Instead: replay the identical (claim, evidence-pool) pairs through both challengers. n=105, seeded.

independentself (= extractor)
Found fault86/105 · 81.9%98/105 · 93.3%
Mean reasoning score0.7270.550

Paired: both 86 · only-independent 0 · only-self 12 · McNemar p = 0.0005.

The hypothesis is refuted and the effect runs the other way. Not one claim in 105 where only the independent challenger objected. D021 corrected in place, not buried.

State the confound yourself: this isolates the input, not the model. The 2×2 crossover I then ran was underpowered (±20 pp cells) — but its main effect confirmed the diagnosis: critic harshness 57.9% vs 25.8%, three times the interaction.
Evaluation · 6 of 6 · the second result I did not want

The loop does not converge — and freezing was hiding it

Every multi-round measurement was confounded: each round also ran fresh retrieval. So: freeze the evidence pool and re-challenge the same claims for 5 passes. Two conditions, because stability_rounds makes churn look bounded by construction.

Pass12345
freeze on — revisions56565
freeze off — revisions66465

Neither converges. Flat ~50% keep rate against evidence that never changed. 6 of 12 claims oscillate — returning to a wording they already held.

stability_rounds is a circuit breaker, not a convergence mechanism: 6 claims froze, and the remaining 6 fell to a 0% keep rate by pass 4.

It invalidated a scheduler I had written hours earlier. That scheduler scores allocation on “did the last round change any claim?” — and oscillating claims change every round, forever. It would have poured the whole budget into the sub-question least able to use it. Caught only because the experiment ran before the scheduler shipped.

The reframe: oscillation is diagnostic, not a bug. A claim that cannot settle means the evidence does not determine the answer. The system now distinguishes three honest states — supported, retracted, and genuinely contested.

…including my own tooling

Three experiment scripts printed an automated verdict. All three overstated their own data — “the process settles on its own” when neither arm converged; “partially a memory problem” on a 29-vs-27 difference at n=12; “SELF-AGREEMENT BIAS PRESENT” on an interaction whose two terms had opposite signs.

Same mechanism every time: a threshold written before seeing the data, with no branch for “inconclusive.” A generated conclusion is a claim like any other. Left in the logs rather than quietly fixed.

Weaknesses · unprompted

Where this system is weak

  1. No embeddings anywhere. Character chunking, keyword-only retrieval, round-robin evidence selection, no reranking. tc3 literally compares dense retrieval without using it.
  2. Support rate is saturated at 98–100%, so three separate experiments could not discriminate between arms. The next real experiment isn’t another arm — it’s a harder question.
  3. Multi-round was unreachable under the shipped allocator: 0 of 35 sub-questions crossed the bar, because difficulty saturates at 0.13–0.31 while budget 3 needs 0.667.
  4. Two of four routing knobs are dead config — swept over 715 stored challenges, they change zero decisions across their whole range.
  5. The quality stack is self-referential — verifier, challenger and judge are all LLMs; the judge reuses the challenger’s client.
  6. Prompt injection: measured at 1.8% success (1 of 56) — but only against naive payloads, and 12 of 14 controls sat at the floor.
  7. No parallelism, retries, caching or resume. Rate limits crashed two runs.
  8. No multi-modal output — the brief’s Example 4 is a capability gap.
Lead with the most damaging, not the most defensible. They asked for this unprompted.
Research landscape · take a position

Where this sits, and what I’d change

  • Search-R1 / ReAct (S1) — interleaving is orchestrated by the pipeline, not emitted by one model. Bought: per-step traceability, agent-level ablations. Lost: a learned retrieval policy. The reformulator learns within a run only.
  • Process reward models — Lightman (S2) — the verifier is a hand-built, untrained step-level reward. The evolution traces are the dataset you’d need to train a real one: 570 revisions with before/after text, support scores, and blind judge verdicts.
  • SCoRe (S3) — where does prompting hit its ceiling? Exactly at the boundary on slide 20: a property must hold, rather than usually happen.
  • Snell, test-time compute (S4) — Track A is a direct applied instance, and the measured limitation is the contribution: I know precisely which part I failed to implement well.
  • MARS / debate (S5) — not built. What would change my mind: evidence that debate catches a different error class than per-claim verification.
What I’d do next

Ordered by expected value

  1. A test case that actually stresses the system — every comparison is currently measuring a ceiling. This unblocks Tracks A and C simultaneously.
  2. Embedding-based evidence selection — better design and closes the fundamentals gap.
  3. Decouple the confidence formula from evolution — a real code defect surfaced twice by two different experiments.
  4. Swap the judge to gemini-2.5-pro — 50% → 90% consistency for ~$2 a sweep. Best price/quality trade found.
  5. Adequately powered 2×2 crossover — the one experiment that would settle D026.
  6. Prompt-injection hardening — delimit evidence, strip role markers.
  7. Train a PRM on the accumulated evolution traces — the data already exists.

Three things to remember

  1. Claims are not append-only — they evolve, and the decision to reverse one is arithmetic, not an LLM’s opinion.
  2. When a property must hold, code it; don’t ask a model for it — measured three times. Capability fixes judgement, never compliance.
  3. I measured my own core assumptions and four came back false — corrected in place, not buried. One of them invalidated a component I’d written the same day.

The system’s most valuable output isn’t the report. It’s the disclosed revision history.
